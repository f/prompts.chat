Five words come up again and again when people talk about AI today: **prompt**, **skill**, **agent**, **bot**, and **team**. They are often used loosely, sometimes as if they meant the same thing, and that makes them harder to learn than they need to be. This chapter is a gentle stop before the chapters that follow. We will put the five words in order, from the simplest to the most elaborate, and look at what each one really is underneath.

The order matters, because the five words form a ladder. Each rung keeps everything from the rung below it and adds exactly one new thing. If you understand what gets added at each step, you understand the whole picture.

To keep things down to earth, we will follow one everyday scene all the way up the ladder: **getting dinner on the table**. Anyone who has ever cooked for a family, or been cooked for, already knows how this goes.

## Rung One: The Prompt

Imagine you are hungry and you ask a friend who is a good cook: "Could you tell me how to make a quick tomato soup?" Your friend answers, and that is the end of it. You asked one thing, you got one answer.

That is a **prompt**. It is the text you send to an AI model: a question, a request, an instruction. As we saw in [Understanding AI Models](/book/01-understanding-ai-models), the model reads that text and writes a reply, one small piece at a time, predicting what should come next. Then it stops and waits. It does not go anywhere, open anything, or remember you tomorrow. When a chat app seems to "remember" the earlier part of your conversation, it is because the app quietly sends the whole conversation back to the model with every new message. The model itself starts fresh each time.

Almost everything in this book so far has been about this first rung, and for good reason. Every rung above it is still made of prompts. A clear prompt is the brick that everything else is built from.

## Rung Two: The Skill

Now imagine your friend gets this question every week. Instead of explaining from scratch each time, they write the method down on a recipe card and put it in a box in the kitchen. The card says what the dish is for, which ingredients to use, the steps in order, and the little tricks that make it come out right. Whenever someone wants tomato soup, the card comes out of the box. The rest of the time, it stays put.

That is a **skill**. A skill is a saved, packaged set of instructions, often with a few helper files, that a model can load when a task calls for it. In practice it is usually a small folder. Inside is a main file, often called `SKILL.md`, which starts with a short name and a one-line description, followed by the full method. The folder can also hold examples, checklists, templates, or small scripts.

Here is the clever part of the mechanism. The model does not read every skill all the time; that would fill its context with things it doesn't need. At first it sees only the short descriptions, like the labels on the recipe cards. When a request matches one of them, the full skill is loaded into the context, and the model follows it. A skill is still made of prompts, but they are prompts you wrote once, tested, and saved, so you don't have to type them again.

Here is a small skill a parent or a teacher might write:

```markdown
---
name: homework-helper
description: Help a child with homework by asking guiding questions instead of giving the answer.
---

# Homework Helper

When a child asks for help with homework:
1. Ask what they already know about the question.
2. Give one small hint at a time, never the full answer.
3. If they are stuck after three hints, show a similar example and let them try again.
4. Praise the effort, not just the right answer.
5. Use short sentences and words a ten-year-old knows.
```

Several AI assistants and coding tools, from different companies, can now read skills packaged in folders like this, and many people share them with one another. The details differ from tool to tool, but the idea is the same everywhere: a method, written down once, ready to be picked up when it fits. We look at skills in much more depth in [Agents & Skills](/book/25-agents-and-skills).

## Rung Three: The Agent

So far, the cook only talks. Now imagine you say: "Please get dinner ready for four people by seven o'clock." This time your friend walks into the kitchen. They open the fridge to see what is there, notice there are no onions, check the clock, choose a recipe card, start cooking, taste the soup, add a little salt, taste again, and finally set the table. Nobody told them each step. You gave them a **goal**, and they worked out the steps by looking, acting, and checking.

That is an **agent**. An agent is an AI model working in a **loop**, with **tools**, toward a goal. The tools are the cook's hands: reading a file, searching the web, opening a calendar, running a calculation, sending a draft. The loop is the rhythm of looking, acting, and checking.

The mechanism is simpler than it sounds. A piece of software around the model, which the book calls the [harness](/book/26-the-harnesses), gives the model a goal and a list of tools it may use. The model does not touch anything directly. Instead, it answers with a request such as "use the weather tool for Saturday in Lisbon." The harness runs that tool and puts the result back into the conversation. The model reads the result and decides on the next step. This repeats until the goal is reached, or until a limit says stop. Each turn of that loop is still a prompt and an answer. What makes it an agent is that the answers lead to actions, and the results of those actions lead to the next answer. We met this rhythm in [Loop Engineering](/book/14a-loop-engineering).

You may already have used agents without calling them that. A coding assistant that reads your project, edits a file, runs the tests, and fixes what failed is an agent. So is a research mode in a chat app that searches many pages before writing its report, or a browser assistant that fills in a form for you. Agents often use skills, too: the cook in our kitchen still reaches for the recipe card when it fits.

A prompt can only give you a wrong answer. An agent can take a wrong action, such as deleting the wrong file or sending a message too early. That is why good agents ask before anything that is hard to undo, and why you should give them only the tools the task really needs.

## Rung Four: The Bot

Our cook is very good, but every evening they arrive as if for the first time. They have to ask again who is allergic to peanuts, where the big pot is, and what you ate yesterday. And they only come when you call them.

Now imagine a cook who **lives with the family**. They have a name and a clear job: "the family's dinner planner." They keep a notebook of what they have learned: Ada is allergic to peanuts, Grandpa doesn't like spicy food, Thursday is pizza night. They have a key to the pantry and access to the grocery delivery account. And every Sunday at six, without anyone asking, they plan the week's dinners, check the family calendar for busy evenings, and leave a shopping list on the fridge for a grown-up to approve.

That is a **bot**, in the sense this book uses the word: a **persistent agent**. Everything from the agent rung is still there, the model, the tools, the loop. What the bot adds is **time**. Under the hood, that usually means four extra pieces:

<Callout type="info" title="An old word with a new meaning">
For years, "bot" meant almost any automatic program: a script that posts the weather, a game character, a help-desk window that offers four fixed buttons. Many of those had no AI model at all. In this book, a bot means something more specific: an agent that persists, with its own identity, memory, and routines. When you meet the word elsewhere, it is worth asking which kind is meant.
</Callout>

Because a bot works while nobody is watching, the rules around it matter more than at any lower rung. A good bot drafts and suggests freely, but waits for a person before it sends, buys, deletes, or publishes anything. The chapter on [Always-On Bots](/book/28-persistent-bots) looks closely at memory, routines, and these approvals.

## Rung Five: The Team

One cook can make a family dinner. But think of a busy restaurant kitchen. One person takes the order. One prepares the vegetables. One cooks the main dish. One checks every plate before it leaves the kitchen. Each has a clear job, and work moves between them in a known way: the order ticket goes to the cook, the finished plate goes to the person who checks it. Nobody needs to know everything; each needs to know their part, and what to pass to whom.

That is a **team**: several bots or agents, each with a **role**, linked by **handoffs**, working toward a shared **purpose**. A handoff is the moment one member passes a named piece of work to the next, like an order ticket or a finished plate.

The mechanism is mostly about what each member gets to see. Each member has its own instructions for its role. When it finishes, it hands on a specific result, such as a list, a draft, or a report, and the next member receives only that, not the whole conversation that produced it. This keeps each member focused and makes mistakes easier to trace: if the final dish is too salty, you can check each plate along the line and see where the salt went in.

A school could run a small team like this to put together a weekly newsletter for parents:

## Pick the Simplest Rung That Works

It is tempting to think that higher on the ladder means better. It doesn't. Every rung adds power, and every rung also adds cost, waiting time, and new ways for things to go wrong. A prompt can give a wrong answer. An agent can take a wrong action. A bot can take a wrong action at three in the morning. A team can pass a mistake from member to member until it looks like a fact.

So the wise habit is the one good cooks already have: use the simplest tool that does the job. Don't hire a restaurant kitchen to boil an egg. Start at the bottom of the ladder, and climb one rung only when the rung you are on clearly isn't enough.

If you are not sure which rung you need, you can ask an AI to help you think it through. The prompt below is itself a good example of the first rung:

<TryIt compact prompt={`I want help with this task: \${task:planning healthy school lunches for my two children every week}.

Using this ladder, from simplest to most complex:
1. Prompt: one instruction, one answer
2. Skill: a saved method the AI loads when needed
3. Agent: an AI that uses tools in a loop toward a goal
4. Bot: an agent that stays, with memory and routines
5. Team: several agents or bots with roles and handoffs

Tell me the simplest rung that would work well for my task, and why. Then tell me what would have to change before I needed the next rung up. Use plain, friendly language.`} />

The five words no longer need to blur together. A **prompt** asks. A **skill** remembers how. An **agent** acts. A **bot** stays. A **team** shares the work. And at every rung, the craft is the same one this book began with: say clearly what you want, where the limits are, and how you will know it went well.
