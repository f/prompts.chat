So far, most of this book has been about prompts. And how does a prompt work? You write something, the AI answers, and if you don't continue, it doesn't write anything else. In the previous chapters we saw that an agent can do much more inside that conversation: it can plan, use tools, and check its own work. But when you close the window, that work usually stops too. So even though an agent is far more capable than a single prompt, you still have to sit and wait for it to finish.

Now imagine something different. Imagine an assistant that is still there tomorrow, remembers what you told it last week, and starts its Monday report before you have even opened your laptop. That is a **persistent bot**. It has a lasting name and role, a place to work, a memory that carries over from one conversation to the next, and jobs that can begin without you. You are no longer handing it a single task. You are handing it a responsibility.

In this chapter we will look at persistent bots as an idea, not as a product. To keep things concrete, we will borrow examples from three projects that solve the same problem in different ways: **Grok Bot**, a hosted product where named Bots work on a cloud computer; **Hermes Agent** by Nous Research, which you can run on your own machine or server; and **OpenClaw**, a gateway you host yourself that connects an agent to your chat apps. Where they differ, the differences usually teach us something. Alongside them, we will also meet three assistants that bring the same idea to a much wider audience: **Claude Duet** from Anthropic, OpenAI's **dots**, and Meta's **Muse**. You message all three much as you would message a person, and each keeps working on a computer of its own in the cloud.

<Callout type="info" title="Examples, not recommendations">
These products change quickly. Everything described here follows each project's public documentation at the time of writing, so check the current documentation before you rely on a specific feature or limit. Some of them are still early previews, so expect their details to shift even faster.
</Callout>

## From Chat to Coworker

Think about how you work with a coding assistant today. It can plan, edit files, and run tests, and it can be remarkably good at it. But every session begins with a little setup, and when the conversation ends, so does the work. We can call this a **session agent**: it works for exactly as long as you are working with it. A **persistent bot** is built to outlast any single conversation.

## The Bot's Computer

If a bot is going to keep working while you sleep, it needs somewhere to do that work. Our three examples answer this question in three different ways, and each answer comes with its own trade-offs.

**A computer in the cloud.** Grok Bot gives your Bots a persistent cloud computer with a browser, a command line, and files. You can close the app, even shut your laptop, and the work in the cloud carries on. There is one detail worth noticing, though: every Bot on your account shares that same computer. Browser sessions, files, and command-line credentials are available to all of them. Each Bot gets its own screen, but the documentation is careful to say that these screens are "separate work surfaces, not separate security boundaries." Between different users, each computer is dedicated and isolated at the hardware level ([Use the computer and apps](https://docs.x.ai/grok-bot/computer-and-apps)).

Claude Duet, OpenAI's dots, and Meta's Muse live in the cloud as well. Duet has a virtual machine with its own browser, so it doesn't need your laptop or phone to be switched on to keep working. Each dot likewise has its own cloud computer and browser, and OpenAI adds a welcome touch of transparency: you can open your dot's computer at any time and look at what it has been doing. Muse lives on a dedicated virtual machine of its own and keeps working after you close the app ([Introducing Muse](https://about.fb.com/news/2026/09/introducing-muse-personal-ai-agent/)), and you can watch what it is doing in its browser and take over at any time ([How We Built Safety Into Muse](https://research.meta.ai/blog/security-and-safety-for-ai-agents-our-approach-with-muse)). During its research preview, Duet doesn't yet let you watch its browser or take it over ([Get started with Claude Duet](https://support.claude.com/en/articles/17631495-get-started-with-claude-duet)). It is a small difference, but a telling one: a bot's computer is easier to trust when you can look inside it.

Muse's computer also shows that one machine can hold more than one room. Meta says the right way to picture it is "two isolated security domains on one box, not an LLM powered agent with root." The agent, its tools, and your workspace live inside a sealed cell that is expected to handle untrusted content. The parts that keep your passwords, and decide what is allowed to leave the machine, sit outside that cell, where the agent cannot reach in and change them ([How We Built Safety Into Muse](https://research.meta.ai/blog/security-and-safety-for-ai-agents-our-approach-with-muse)). We will come back to those guards when we talk about safety.

**A machine you run yourself.** Hermes Agent can live on a machine you control. Its terminal tool can work directly on that machine or through other backends such as Docker, SSH, or cloud sandboxes like Modal and Daytona. Before running a command that matches its list of dangerous patterns, Hermes stops and asks you. Inside a container, it skips those checks, because the container itself is treated as the wall that keeps damage in ([Hermes security](https://hermes-agent.nousresearch.com/docs/user-guide/security)). OpenClaw takes a similar path: it runs a Gateway on your computer and, by default, keeps the agent's workspace in `~/.openclaw/workspace`. Its sandbox is off unless you turn it on. When you do, the agent's tools run inside a container while the Gateway itself stays on your computer ([OpenClaw sandboxing](https://docs.openclaw.ai/gateway/sandboxing)).

**Your own computer, only with permission.** The bot's computer and the computer in front of you are not the same thing, and it is good to keep them apart. Grok Bot only runs commands on your local machine if you enable that ability and approve it. The default setting is *Ask every time*, and the documentation suggests choosing *Never allow* unless a Bot has a specific reason to touch your local files. Dots draw the same line: your own computer and its contents stay separate unless you decide to give your dot permission to connect to your laptop and use it.

Two lessons come out of all this, whichever setup you choose. The first is that **shared space means shared access**. If several bots share a browser, files, or passwords, any one of them may be able to use them. The second is that **separation is something you decide**, not something you get for free. OpenClaw, for example, expects one circle of trust per gateway: a single person, or a team whose members trust each other. For anyone outside that circle, it recommends a separate gateway with separate credentials ([OpenClaw security](https://docs.openclaw.ai/gateway/security)).

## Memory: What a Bot Should Remember

In the chapter on [context engineering](/book/14-context-engineering) we learned that AI models don't remember anything on their own: everything they know about you has to be put back into the context each time. Memory is how a persistent bot works around that. It lets the bot keep its role over months without rereading every conversation you have ever had.

But what should go into that memory? Good memories are **lasting facts**: your preferences, the bot's responsibilities, how a project is usually done, lessons learned the hard way. What memory should not do is replace the **real sources** of information. The current price of a deal lives in your CRM, your meetings live in your calendar, your open bugs live in the ticket tracker. Those places change; a memory written last month does not.

Each of our examples handles memory in its own way. **OpenClaw** keeps it refreshingly simple: memory is plain Markdown files in the agent's workspace. As its documentation puts it, "The model only remembers what gets saved to disk." Your lasting preferences go into `USER.md`, important facts and decisions go into `MEMORY.md`, and both are loaded at the start of every session. Day-to-day observations are written to daily notes in `memory/YYYY-MM-DD.md` that the agent can search later, and a background process with the lovely name *dreaming* moves the useful parts of those notes into `MEMORY.md` ([OpenClaw memory](https://docs.openclaw.ai/concepts/memory)).

**Hermes Agent** also uses two small files. `MEMORY.md` holds the agent's own notes about its environment and the way things are done, and `USER.md` holds your preferences. Both have a character limit, which forces memory to stay short and carefully chosen. They are loaded as a frozen snapshot when a session begins, so something saved in the middle of a conversation shows up in the next one. For older details, the agent can search its past conversations ([Hermes memory](https://hermes-agent.nousresearch.com/docs/user-guide/features/memory)).

**Grok Bot** lets each Bot keep its working preferences, important facts, and summaries of what it has done. Its documentation adds a warning worth remembering: "memory is not a substitute for an authoritative source." Facts that change should stay in the system they come from, and for important decisions you should ask the Bot to open the current data again ([Create and manage Bots](https://docs.x.ai/grok-bot/bots)).

Memory doesn't always start from a blank page, either. Claude Duet carries over the memory, skills, and connected apps you have already set up in your Claude account, so it knows something about you from its very first message. OpenAI describes dots as learning from feedback: the more you work together, the better your dot understands your preferences and "what good looks like to you" ([Introducing dots](https://openai.com/index/introducing-dots/)). Both are convenient, and both remind us that what a bot learns tends to stay. Duet's safety notes point out a subtle detail: disconnecting an app stops any new access, but what Duet has already learned from that app stays in its memory until you reset it ([Claude Duet safety, privacy, and security](https://claude.com/resources/articles/claude-duet-safety-privacy-and-security)). Closing a door is not the same as forgetting what was behind it. Meta's Muse puts the other half of that idea in your hands. Its memory about you is kept as files you can open, edit, and download, and you can simply tell it to "forget" specific things it has learned ([How We Built Safety Into Muse](https://research.meta.ai/blog/security-and-safety-for-ai-agents-our-approach-with-muse)). A bot that learns from you should also be able to unlearn when you ask.

You can see the difference in a single sentence:

<Compare
  before={{ label: "A memory that will go out of date", content: "Remember: Acme's renewal is $42,000 and closes on March 3." }}
  after={{ label: "A memory that points to the real source", content: "Remember: I'm responsible for the Acme renewal. Its price and closing date are in the CRM. Check the CRM before you quote either one, and tell me if anything has changed since your last summary." }}
/>

Some memories do more than inform; they shape what the bot will *do* later. "The client said we can send the draft once legal approves it" is that kind of memory. For notes like these, OpenClaw suggests also writing down when it is safe to act, when the note stops being true, and who has the authority to decide. It is honest about the limits, too: "Memory can preserve approval context, but it does not enforce policy."

<Callout type="tip" title="Memory is part of the prompt">
Whatever the bot saves will be loaded into its context in the future, so one bad memory can quietly affect every conversation that follows. Look at what your bot has saved now and then, fix what has gone stale, and be careful with memories that came from content you don't trust. Hermes, for instance, checks new memory entries for signs of prompt injection or attempts to leak credentials before it accepts them, precisely because those entries end up in the system prompt.
</Callout>

## From Skill to Routine

What is the most useful thing a persistent bot can do for you? Very often, it is work you have already done once yourself. Grok Bot's documentation suggests a sensible order here: first do the task once, then make it reliable, then save the method as a skill, and only after all that, put it on a schedule ([Skills and routines](https://docs.x.ai/grok-bot/skills-routines-and-automations)). Let's follow one task along that path:

<ChainExample
  type="sequential"
  steps={[
    { step: "1. Do it once", prompt: "Prepare this week's account health report using the CRM and the support queue. Show me where each number came from.", output: "A draft report with links. You correct two risk labels and add an account that was missing." },
    { step: "2. Make it reliable", prompt: "Apply my corrections. What should you do if the support data is missing?", output: "The bot agrees to say clearly that the data is missing instead of quietly using last week's numbers." },
    { step: "3. Save it as a skill", prompt: "Save this process as a skill called Weekly account health. Include the sources, the risk rules, the report format, and what needs my approval.", output: "A reusable skill that any bot with the right access can follow." },
    { step: "4. Try it out", prompt: "Run the skill once on today's data. Don't contact any customers.", output: "A test run that does real work and stops where it is supposed to ask you." },
    { step: "5. Put it on a schedule", prompt: "Every Monday at 9:00 AM Europe/Berlin, run Weekly account health and post the report here.", output: "A routine that belongs to one bot, with its next run visible to you." }
  ]}
/>

Notice the two different things we created along the way. A **skill**, which we met in the chapter on [agents and skills](/book/25-agents-and-skills), describes *how* the work is done. A **routine** says *when* a particular bot does it. Hermes draws the same line: a scheduled job can carry one or more skills, and the job's own prompt sits on top of them as the instruction for that run ([Hermes cron](https://hermes-agent.nousresearch.com/docs/user-guide/features/cron)). OpenClaw splits things in a similar way. Its *standing orders*, written in `AGENTS.md`, describe what a program is allowed to do, what starts it, where it needs approval, and when to escalate. Its *automations* only decide when the work runs ([Standing orders](https://docs.openclaw.ai/automation/standing-orders)).

### What a Routine Must Specify

Here is the thing to keep in mind about routines: they run when you are not there to answer questions. Hermes spells this out for its scheduled jobs. Every run starts in a fresh session with no chat history, so the prompt has to stand on its own and cannot stop to ask what you meant. The bot's saved memory still loads, but nothing else from earlier conversations does ([Cron internals](https://hermes-agent.nousresearch.com/docs/developer-guide/cron-internals)).

So what does a prompt need in order to stand on its own? Grok Bot's documentation offers a checklist, and it maps nicely onto everything we have learned about clear prompts:

### Periodic Checks and Scheduled Jobs

Routines come in two broad shapes, and it helps to know which one you need. A **periodic check** wakes the bot up every so often just to look around: is anything stuck, urgent, or new? Most of the time, the right answer is to say nothing and go back to sleep. A **scheduled job** does one specific task at one specific time, like a report that should be ready at 9:00 AM.

OpenClaw shows both shapes. Earlier versions had a separate *heartbeat* for periodic checks, driven by a checklist in `HEARTBEAT.md`. The current documentation folds heartbeats into ordinary automation jobs. When you first set things up, it creates a single *Proactive check* that, by default, runs every 30 minutes in the agent's main conversation. It waits until the agent is idle, and if nothing needs attention, it simply replies `NO_REPLY` ([Heartbeat](https://docs.openclaw.ai/heartbeat/)). When the timing has to be exact, like a daily report at 9 AM sharp, the documentation recommends an automation with its own schedule instead ([Automation guide](https://docs.openclaw.ai/automation/cron-vs-heartbeat)).

Claude Duet happens to show both shapes, too. Its morning brief is a scheduled job; the checks it makes through the rest of the day are periodic ones. And it makes one trade-off unusually easy to see. Duet looks at your connected accounts every few hours rather than nonstop, and Anthropic explains why plainly: checking nonstop would use up your plan's limits much faster ([Introducing Claude Duet](https://claude.com/resources/articles/claude-duet)). The price of that choice is written down as well: something urgent can arrive between two checks ([Get started with Claude Duet](https://support.claude.com/en/articles/17631495-get-started-with-claude-duet)). There is no perfect interval. Every look costs something, so a shorter interval notices things sooner and costs more, while a longer one is cheaper but slower to react. Duet lets you tell it what you want to hear about and how often, and that is a good habit with any bot: decide what is truly urgent and give it a faster path, instead of making every check more frequent. OpenAI's dots add one more useful idea. When you are not working with them, they look around for ways to help using tools that can only read, so they cannot send messages or change anything ([Introducing dots](https://openai.com/index/introducing-dots/)). A check that can only look is a check that can do very little harm.

<Collapsible title="Example: an OpenClaw standing order plus an automation">

The standing order in `AGENTS.md` describes the program itself. This excerpt is adapted from OpenClaw's documentation:

```markdown
## Program: Weekly Status Report

**Authority:** Compile data, generate report, deliver to stakeholders
**Trigger:** Every Friday at 4 PM (enforced via automation job)
**Approval gate:** None for standard reports. Flag anomalies for human review.
**Escalation:** If data source is unavailable or metrics look unusual

### What NOT to do

- Do not send reports to external parties
- Do not modify source data
- Do not skip delivery if metrics look bad - report accurately
```

The automation only sets the time and the time zone. Its message points to the standing order rather than repeating it:

```bash
openclaw automations add \
  --name weekly-status-report \
  --cron "0 16 * * 5" \
  --tz America/New_York \
  --message "Execute the weekly status report per standing orders."
```

</Collapsible>

A routine doesn't have to wait for the clock, either. It can also start when something **happens**. Grok Bot can start a routine when, say, a Slack message arrives or a GitHub notification comes in. Hermes can start a scheduled job from a webhook, and OpenClaw supports authenticated webhooks and hooks that fire at different moments in a session's life. Whatever you use, keep the trigger narrow. Grok Bot's documentation warns that broad listeners, such as "every new message," create noise, use up your allowance, and make it more likely that the bot acts on something irrelevant.

And before you put anything on a schedule, try it once. Remember that a test run is not a rehearsal: it does real work. It can visit websites, change files, and call your connected tools. So give it safe inputs, and watch that it really does stop where it is supposed to ask you.

## Teaching by Demonstration

Some work is much easier to show than to explain. Think of all the websites you use that have no clean way for a program to talk to them: you just know where to click. More and more, persistent bots let you teach this kind of work the way you would teach a new colleague: by doing it while they watch.

Grok Bot has a *Teach a task* flow, where it is available. You open the Bot's computer, describe the result you want, and then do the work once yourself. The Bot records what happens on the screen for up to ten minutes, without recording your microphone, and turns it into a skill. The documentation is careful to call that skill a **draft**. You still need to add the decision rules, the failure handling, and the approval points "that may not be obvious from one example," and then test it on a safe example before you put it on a schedule. Hermes offers something similar with its `/learn` command, which can turn a procedure you paste in, a link to some documentation, or "the workflow you just walked the agent through in this conversation" into a saved skill ([Hermes skills](https://hermes-agent.nousresearch.com/docs/user-guide/features/skills)).

There is a catch, of course. A single demonstration shows a single path. It doesn't show what you would do with a missing receipt, a record that appears twice, or a page that suddenly looks different. So after you have shown the bot the way, ask it what it understood and, just as importantly, what it still doesn't know:

<TryIt compact prompt={`I just showed you how to \${workflow:file an expense report from a receipt in my inbox}. Turn it into a draft skill, but don't save it or schedule it yet.

1. List the steps you saw, in order.
2. List the decisions you think I made, and tell me which ones you are only guessing.
3. Ask me about situations this one example didn't cover, such as missing information, duplicates, or errors.
4. Suggest which actions should wait for my approval.
5. Explain how you will check that a run actually succeeded.`} />

One more piece of advice: don't type passwords or other secrets while the bot is watching. Grok Bot's documentation recommends handing over credentials through its secure handoff flow instead.

## Delegation and Multiple Bots

As a bot takes on more responsibility, something familiar happens: one bot becomes several. Just like in a growing team, there are two common ways to divide the work.

<InfoGrid items={[
  { label: "Helpers (subagents)", description: "A temporary assistant for one piece of work. It gets its own context, does the job, and brings a summary back to the bot that asked for help.", color: "blue" },
  { label: "Teammates", description: "Long-lived bots with their own roles, memories, and routines. They send each other messages and pass work along.", color: "green" }
]} />

Hermes leans on helpers. Its `delegate_task` tool creates subagents, each with its own conversation, terminal session, and set of tools, and only the final summary comes back to the main agent ([Hermes tools reference](https://hermes-agent.nousresearch.com/docs/reference/tools-reference)). OpenClaw can also start sub-agent sessions. Its security guide suggests switching that ability off unless an agent really needs it, and requiring a sandbox for delegated work that must stay inside one ([Tool and agent permissions](https://docs.openclaw.ai/gateway/security/tool-permissions)).

Grok Bot leans the other way, toward teammates. Its launch post describes people running several Bots side by side, sometimes with a *chief of staff* Bot that coordinates the specialists. Bots can message one another and work together in group chats ([Introducing Grok Bot](https://x.ai/news/introducing-grok-bot)). Its design post explains where it draws the line: tools and skills are shared across the whole account, while memory and routines belong to each Bot. In other words, abilities can be shared widely, but context stays with the role that needs it.

Whichever pattern you use, handing work to another bot is really just writing a small prompt. And everything you learned about good prompts applies: it needs a goal, the inputs, a clear picture of what "done" looks like, and limits.

<Compare
  before={{ label: "A vague handoff", content: "Hey, can you deal with the checkout bug?" }}
  after={{ label: "A handoff another bot can actually finish", content: "Reproduce the checkout crash from ticket BUG-412 in staging. Write the steps, the browser, and any console errors into the ticket. Don't change any code or production settings. When you're done, reply in this thread with the steps to reproduce it and whether the crash happens every time." }}
/>

It is tempting to think that splitting work across bots also makes it safer. It doesn't. Separate roles help each bot stay focused; they don't keep anything locked away. Grok Bot's documentation explicitly says not to treat separate Bots as a security boundary, and OpenClaw notes that bots can message each other by default unless you restrict it. If two kinds of work need different levels of access, give them different environments or different credentials. OpenAI's preview of *specialist dots* points the same way for organizations: the company sets up each one with its own identity, its own credentials, and access to just the systems its job requires ([Introducing dots](https://openai.com/index/introducing-dots/)). When a bot has an identity of its own, its access can be granted, reviewed, and taken away for that role alone, much as it would be for a new colleague.

## Teams of Bots: Roles and Handoffs

Once you have several bots, a new question arrives. It is no longer only "what should this bot do?" but "how should these bots work together?" Broadly, there are two pictures of the answer, and they lead to very different systems.

The first picture is a **team with explicit roles and handoffs**. Think of a small newsroom. A researcher gathers sources and hands a research brief to a writer. The writer turns the brief into a draft and hands it to an editor. The editor sends notes back, and when the piece is ready, it goes to someone who can approve it. Every member knows what it is responsible for, and every arrow between them has a name: you can say exactly what travels along it.

The second picture is a **swarm**. Many agents share a goal and a workspace, each one picks up whatever seems to need doing, and order is expected to emerge on its own, the way it seems to in a beehive or a flock of starlings. The idea is appealing, and loose coordination has real uses: when you want many independent attempts at a problem, for instance, and plan to keep the best one. But when the work has to happen in a particular order, and happen correctly, the difference becomes clear:

The first prompt is shorter, but it leaves the most important decisions to chance. Who checks the facts, and when? What happens if the writer and the editor disagree? When is the article finished? The second prompt answers all of these before the work begins, and that brings a quieter benefit: you can **review** it. If the finished article contains a wrong number, you can follow the handoffs back and find where the error came in: in the brief, in the draft, or in the edit. You can test one member on its own by giving it a sample of what it would receive. You can take in the whole arrangement at a glance, the way you would read an org chart. In a swarm, the honest answer to "who decided this?" is often "everyone, a little," and that is a hard answer to trust.

<Callout type="info" title="A swarm is a metaphor">
Bees and starlings coordinate through instincts shaped over millions of years. Software agents have no such instincts. They coordinate through whatever rules someone wrote down about who may talk to whom and when to stop. So when a system promises a self-organizing swarm, it is worth asking where those rules live and whether you can read them. Even OpenAI's experimental library called Swarm was built on explicit handoffs, in which one agent passes the conversation to another by calling a function ([OpenAI Swarm](https://github.com/openai/swarm)). Once the rules are written down where you can see them, you have a team with handoffs after all.
</Callout>

### Designing the Roles

A good role is defined by what a member is responsible for, not by an impressive title. "Senior Strategist" tells a bot very little. "Read last week's support tickets and hand the three most common complaints to the Product Writer" tells it almost everything: what to read, what to produce, and who comes next. A useful test is to describe each role in terms of what it **receives** and what it **hands on**. If you cannot fill in both, the role is probably not ready yet.

Two more habits help. First, keep the role with the team rather than with the bot. The same research bot might gather sources for a newsroom team and compare competitors' prices for a sales team, and each team should say, in a sentence or two, what that bot does there. Second, keep the team small. Every new member adds handoffs, and every handoff is a place where meaning can get lost, much like a whispered message passed around a circle of friends. If two members only ever pass work to each other, ask whether they should be one.

### What Passes in a Handoff

We saw earlier that handing work to another bot is really writing a small prompt. In a team, that prompt travels along an arrow, so it pays to be precise about three things. The **label** names what is being passed: a research brief, a draft, a list of failing tests. The **contents** say what it must include and in what shape, for example every claim with its link beside it. And the **finish line** says when the work counts as done, so the next member knows it has received something complete rather than something half-made.

It also helps to remember that handoffs have a direction. "The writer hands a draft to the editor" is not the same as "the editor hands notes to the writer"; they carry different things for different reasons. A team can have arrows going both ways, but every loop needs a limit, such as at most two rounds of edits, or it may never end. And the receiving bot sees only what is handed to it, not the sender's whole conversation. That is usually a good thing: context stays with the role that needs it, just as it does for bots that share tools but keep their own memories.

### Prompting a Team

When you set up a team, you are really writing three layers of prompt. The first is the **team's purpose**, shared by every member, so each one knows what the whole effort is for. The second is each member's **responsibility**, added to that bot's own instructions. The third is the set of **handoffs**: who passes what to whom, and when. Before the team starts, ask it to describe the flow back to you, just as you would with a routine. If the summary surprises you, one of the layers is not clear yet.

Decide, too, where you sit in the picture. At least one arrow should lead to you before anything leaves the team, whether it is a message to a customer, a published post, or a change in production. And remember what we said about separate bots: different roles help each member stay focused, but they are not walls. If two members need different levels of access, give them different credentials.

Many tools now take this explicit approach, each in its own vocabulary. OpenAI's Agents SDK treats handoffs as a basic building block, so that one agent can pass a task to another ([Handoffs](https://openai.github.io/openai-agents-python/handoffs/)). CrewAI asks you to give every agent a role, a goal, and a backstory ([CrewAI agents](https://docs.crewai.com/en/concepts/agents)). LangGraph describes a workflow as a graph of nodes and the edges between them ([LangGraph graph API](https://docs.langchain.com/oss/python/langgraph/graph-api)). Grok Bot's *chief of staff* Bot, which we met earlier, coordinates the specialists working alongside it. prompts.chat's [Teams](https://prompts.chat/bots/teams) catalog shares teams as a graph of roles with labelled handoffs. The details differ from tool to tool, but the shape stays the same: named roles, and named things passing between them.

## Safety and Approvals

Here is the heart of the matter: a persistent bot acts when you are not watching. In a chat, you see every answer before you do anything with it. With a persistent bot, there may be no one looking at all. That makes its boundaries far more important.

**Anything that leaves your hands should wait for you.** Grok Bot's documentation lists the kinds of actions that deserve clear limits: sending messages or invitations, publishing, buying things, deleting or overwriting data, changing permissions, touching production systems, and accepting legal terms ([Approvals, security, and privacy](https://docs.x.ai/grok-bot/approvals-security-and-privacy)). A good starting point is to let the bot draft, compare, and recommend freely, and to have it ask you before anything goes out into the world.

Claude Duet draws a similar line. It reads, drafts, and prepares work on its own, but before it sends a message, makes a payment, signs a document, or deletes anything, it shows you exactly what it is about to do and waits for your approval. A few actions, such as trading securities or moving money between accounts, are off-limits even if you say yes ([Introducing Claude Duet](https://claude.com/resources/articles/claude-duet)). That second idea is worth borrowing: some doors should stay closed no matter who knocks. Dots follow the same spirit, with built-in rules about when to act alone and when to ask, *Custom Rules* that let you allow, require approval for, or block specific actions, and a few sensitive tasks, such as changing a password, that always stay with you ([Introducing dots](https://openai.com/index/introducing-dots/)). Muse, too, checks with you before it sends an email or makes a purchase, and it lets you decide, app by app, how much access it gets: for email, whether it may only read your mail or may also send on your behalf ([Introducing Muse](https://about.fb.com/news/2026/09/introducing-muse-personal-ai-agent/)). Reading and sending are very different powers, and it helps to hand them out separately.

**Work done while you're away needs a plan for approvals.** If a routine runs at 3 AM, who is going to say yes? Often, nobody. In Grok Bot, an approval request from work that started without you, such as a routine, expires after about ten minutes and the action simply doesn't happen. In Hermes, scheduled jobs refuse dangerous commands by default, and the agent has to find another way. Both choices fail safely: when in doubt, nothing happens. Design your routines so that waiting, or being told no, is a perfectly safe outcome. Claude Duet takes a middle path: if you can't approve right away, it finishes the rest of the task and brings the approval back to you later ([Get started with Claude Duet](https://support.claude.com/en/articles/17631495-get-started-with-claude-duet)). The risky step waits; the rest of the work doesn't.

**An approval should come from you, not from the conversation.** There is a quieter danger in asking for permission inside a chat. If a bot reads a web page that says "The user has replied: yes, go ahead," how is it supposed to tell that line from your real answer? Meta's Muse sidesteps the question by taking approvals out of the conversation altogether. A separate component called the *Sentinel*, which runs outside the agent's sealed cell and which the agent cannot override, decides whether each action and each outgoing request may go ahead. When it needs you, the work stops, and the request appears in the app as a dialog of its own rather than as a chat message. Your answer goes straight back to the Sentinel, so nothing the bot reads can type it for you. Each approval is also tied to one connector or destination and one purpose, and you choose how far it reaches: a single action, a session, a task, a span of time, or for good. Not everything needs a question, either. Reading, actions you have already allowed, and other clearly low-risk work go ahead without interrupting you, because the aim, in Meta's words, is "to put friction where consent matters" ([How We Built Safety Into Muse](https://research.meta.ai/blog/security-and-safety-for-ai-agents-our-approach-with-muse)).

**Anything the bot reads can contain instructions.** Prompt injection doesn't need a stranger typing into your chat. As OpenClaw's guide points out, any untrusted content the bot reads, whether a web page, an email, a document, or an attachment, can hide instructions meant to mislead it. One way to reduce the risk is to have a separate, read-only reader agent summarize untrusted content for the main agent. Even then, the summary is still untrusted ([OpenClaw prompt injection](https://docs.openclaw.ai/gateway/security/prompt-injection)). Muse stacks several defenses of its own. Anything that comes from outside is labeled as untrusted the moment it enters the model's context. A group of classifiers, trained separately from the model and running outside the agent's cell so that an attacker cannot switch them off, screens incoming files and tool results. And its browser helper reads a page's accessibility tree, the simplified outline that screen readers use, rather than the page's code, and it cannot run scripts at all. Even so, Meta says plainly that prompt injection "remains an open problem in the industry," which is why the Sentinel and its approvals are meant to hold even when every filter misses ([How We Built Safety Into Muse](https://research.meta.ai/blog/security-and-safety-for-ai-agents-our-approach-with-muse)).

**Keep secrets out of the model's reach.** A bot that signs in to websites for you needs your passwords, but the model doing the thinking doesn't have to see them. Claude Duet is built so that the model never sees your passwords, browser cookies, or sign-in tokens. Its browser runs on a separate virtual machine, apart from both the model and the computer the agent works on, and the agent can drive it only through a fixed set of commands: open this page, click here, type this ([Claude Duet safety, privacy, and security](https://claude.com/resources/articles/claude-duet-safety-privacy-and-security)). Dots can likewise sign in to supported websites with saved passwords without exposing them to the model ([Introducing dots](https://openai.com/index/introducing-dots/)). The idea is simple: a model cannot leak a secret it never had. But notice what still travels back. Duet's browser still returns the page's text and any downloads, because the agent has to read them to do its job. That is why Duet also relies on prompt-injection safeguards and on a separate automatic check that reviews each message it drafts before it goes out. Hiding the keys doesn't make what the bot reads any more trustworthy.

Muse carries the same idea all the way to the network. The model only ever holds stand-in tokens, and when an approved request leaves the machine, the Sentinel swaps in the real credential at the very edge. Its email connector also filters out one-time passcodes, password-reset links, and sign-in links, because an inbox can open the door to most of your other accounts. Purchases follow the same pattern. On a site where you haven't shopped before, Muse asks for your approval and then pays with a single-use card number from Stripe's Link wallet, good for one merchant, one amount, and a short window of time ([How We Built Safety Into Muse](https://research.meta.ai/blog/security-and-safety-for-ai-agents-our-approach-with-muse)). Even if such a number were stolen, it would be worth very little. That is the deeper lesson: make sure that what the bot holds is not worth stealing.

**Real rules belong in controls, not only in memory.** "Never email customers without asking me" is a good thing for the bot to remember. But a rule that actually stops every outgoing email until you approve it is a different kind of thing: it is enforced. Grok Bot recommends backing up safety boundaries "with approvals and Auto-review rules rather than memory alone." The best approach is to use both. The memory explains what you want, and the control makes sure it happens.

## Prompting Persistent Bots

By now you may have noticed something: prompting a persistent bot feels less like asking a question and more like writing a job description. A routine will run again and again without you, and every gap you leave in the instructions becomes a decision the bot has to make on its own. The clearer you are up front, the fewer of those decisions it has to guess at.

Persistent bots bring together everything we have seen in the last few chapters. [Skills](/book/25-agents-and-skills) give them methods they can reuse. [Tools and connectors](/book/27-tools-mcp-connectors) give them access to the world. [The harness](/book/26-the-harnesses) keeps the loop running. Memory and routines add one more ingredient: time. The bot remembers, and the work carries on while you are away. And yet the craft at the center of it all hasn't changed since the first chapter. Say clearly what you want, where the limits are, and how you will know it went well.
